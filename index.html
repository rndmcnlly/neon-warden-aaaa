NEON WARDEN

A short vertical shoot-'em-up. Fly a contractor's ship up the lanes for pay. The Warden waits at the end. Do you have to fight it?

Play in your browser

Opens the game on its own page: press any key or tap, two short logos, then the main menu. Sound is on (adjustable). Loads up to about 25 MB as you play; nothing to install.

  • Free
  • No account, ads or tracking
  • Keyboard, mouse or touch
  • English, Español, 中文

Content note: ship combat, deaths and a morally gray ending. The story is the same on every device.

01 A slice is an argument

A vertical slice is made for the people deciding whether a game should exist: publishers, funders, and in a classroom, graders and playtesters. Its include and exclude decisions are the real design document. This one inverts the usual priority: the front end every player touches (boot, menus, settings, subtitles, language, accessibility) is near shipping quality, while features outside the slice (multiplayer, store, leaderboards, cloud saves, photo mode) are visible but say so plainly.

Human decisions. Adam reframed the project from parody to an authentic slice after playing a parody prototype, kept the pilot off-screen as a scope cut, and decided that school devices get the same story with no softened wording.

What went wrong. An AI reviewer suggested hiding the unavailable menu grid to imply completeness; we declined it as overclaiming. The pilot scope cut left a dangling reference that broke the full typecheck until a later integration repaired it.

Evidence: PRODUCTION.md (Built and Stubbed table), vertical-slice checklist research, AGENTS.md (dated decisions).

02 Assets are programs

No asset is hand-edited in a tool and saved as the source of truth. Every class of asset is regenerated from text by a script that runs outside the game, and the game only reads the outputs and their manifests. Ships, enemies and sector scenery are Python scripts run by Blender headlessly (a full rebuild takes seconds) and exported as glTF. Every spoken line lives in one trilingual script file (speaker, English, Spanish, Chinese, direction); a local speech model renders it, a local recognition pass checks it, and subtitle and word timings come from the rendered audio, so any line can be rewritten and regenerated. Music and interface sounds come from local generation, auditioned as candidates and then promoted with measured loudness and beat grids.

What went wrong. Varying random seeds produced near-identical candidates; varying the written brief produced real choices. A word-timing format mismatch between the voice pipeline and the subtitle code broke word highlighting even though each side passed its own checks.

Evidence: art/blender/README.md, vo/render.py, sfx/README.md, music/README.md.

03 Engine and platform

The original was a small Phaser game written as a course starter. This slice is a full rewrite in three.js and strict TypeScript (everything except shaders), run with Deno tasks and built by Vite into a plain static site: no server, no accounts, nothing to keep alive. The simulation is pure and deterministic; rendering, audio and menus sit around it. The game page is an installable offline web app: installing it and downloading it for offline play are separate, explicit steps, and an update never reloads a run in progress. It is laid out portrait-first for phones, with touch controls that express the full fire verb, and a low graphics tier for school Chromebooks.

What went wrong. Mobile, touch and school-device support arrived late, after the desktop front end, which is the reverse of what a phone-first audience needs. The offline panel still has English-only labels. Moving the game to its own page for this site exposed a logo path that only worked at a domain root.

Evidence: docs/PWA.md, CONTRACTS.md, vite.config.ts, src/platform/.

04 Two kinds of AI

Generative AI did two different jobs here, and they should not be confused. Frontier models in the cloud (Claude Opus 5.5, with GPT-6.1 Sol for one segment while a usage window was exhausted) acted as production orchestration and implementation agents: planning, dispatching parallel workers with file-scoped ownership, writing the code, tests, tools and documentation. Mostly local models on a laptop made assets: Qwen3-TTS for voices, Qwen3-ASR to check them, Stable Audio 3 for music. A few gameplay sound effects came from a cloud model (ElevenLabs Sound Effects via fal). Auxiliary roles: Gemini 4 Argon as a blind audiovisual judge with zero data retention, and small models (GLM-5.3-Flash, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash) for research summaries and compressing agent instructions.

Human decisions. Story direction, the scope cuts, voice casting picks made by ear in context, and every playtest that redirected the work were Adam's. The credits say "Adam Smith, and his coupled agents", and name each model's role.

What went wrong. Agents that re-read large sources burned through context; one shared status file was truncated by a concurrent write and rebuilt from memory. Both became written rules for the next agents.

Evidence: src/ui/creditsRoll.ts, docs/SKILLS.md, docs/INTEGRATION-QUEUE.md.

05 QA at many levels

The simulation has no clock and no hidden randomness, and the thing that flies the ship is an interchangeable "pilot": a human, or an autopilot that fights or never fires. That one design choice, inherited from the original course starter, makes QA cheap at every scale: unit tests on pure functions, seeded replays that must hash the same, autopilots playing the whole slice at many times real speed, URL flags that skip straight to any scene, recorded reels, and human playtests.

What went wrong. For a while every recorded reel was silent, because browser screen recording drops audio; a judge asked to review sound could not have heard anything. A real audio tap fixed it and immediately exposed voice lines that were being dropped on skip paths.

Evidence: qa/README.md, src/contracts/flags.ts, src/contracts/pilot.ts, tests/.

06 Dailies and blind judges

Short, single-topic reels (menus, a combat beat, the boss, an ending) worked like film dailies: watched the same day, compared with the last cut. Some were shown to an AI judge blind, with no script, mechanics or intent: first describe what happened, what was heard and what was unclear; only then give scores or advice. Its account was then checked against the game's event log and audio stems. Detection is not understanding: noticing a bright threat is not the same as knowing why a life was lost.

What went wrong, usefully. After a night of fixes meant to make the core loop clearer, the blind judge preferred the before build in 7 of 10 pairs. We cut the added floor clutter and did not claim an improvement. Judges also "heard" subtitles in silent windows and invented signage; those claims are kept in tables of rejected claims next to the ones adopted.

Evidence: intelligibility study, night verification, review technique.

07 Choosing in context

Candidates are judged where they will be used, not in isolation: a voice take inside its scene with the music and effects under it, switchable by a URL flag in the running game. Choosing never destroys the alternative: rejected takes, candidate music and voice variants stay addressable, and timing fixes such as a voice's start offset are stored as data rather than baked into audio. Reviews are kept quick: one clip at a time, evidence revealed after watching, a clear next and previous.

Human decisions. After hearing both in context, Adam chose a different tutorial voice than the original default; the old one remains one flag away.

Evidence: src/audio/variants.ts, audition technique, scripts/vo_onset.ts.

08 Read the source

Start with src/scenes/index.ts: one function maps game events to scenes and is the whole slice's structure. The simulation is in src/sim/, rendering in src/render/, menus in src/ui/, and the contracts between them in src/contracts/. Research notes, each with a framing question, options and what was declined, live in research/.

What you can regenerate. Models (with Blender) and the text tables (with Deno) rebuild from the repository alone. Voices and music need the local models named above; the cloud sound effects need a paid account. So the generated audio is committed, and the project runs from a static build with nothing to maintain.
