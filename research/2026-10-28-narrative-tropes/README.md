# Narrative tropes for Neon Warden: a TV Tropes survey and three story spines

**Framing question.** Which established game-narrative tropes (as catalogued on TV Tropes) best serve the five required beats of the Neon Warden vertical slice (logos, overlong tutorial with a tutorial AI, gameplay with a flashback on the first powerup, an over-acted Warden boss, a morally gray ending that motivates a pacifist run), and what single narrative spine should the script-as-data VO file be written against? Secondary question: is "Pilot" the right name for the silent protagonist?

**Inputs.** [`AGENTS.md`](../../AGENTS.md) (required beats, tone: authentic, high-polish, self-aware, not a parody) and the [original-reference teardown](../2026-10-28-original-reference/README.md), especially its narrative hooks: "the lanes stay closed" / "the lanes are open", the Warden's 150-tick invulnerable descent, the Warden summoning drones (its own scouts), the elegiac three-line epilogue, the `Pilot` interface (keyboard or autopilot), and the autopilot silently buying a continue whenever it dies.

## Findings: trope catalogue

Flags: **Use** (play it straight, with polish), **Subvert** (set it up, then turn it), **Avoid** (deliberately leave it out, with the reason).

### 1. The player character

- **[Heroic Mime](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/HeroicMime)** (Silent Protagonist redirects here). A protagonist who never speaks, while the rest of the cast reacts as if they had. Examples: Red's ellipses in Pokémon Gold/Silver *(on page)*; Link; Gordon Freeman *(from memory)*; Ace Combat's callsign aces (Mobius 1, Cipher, Trigger) *(from memory)*, which are the closest genre match. **Use.** Pilot never speaks a single VO line. All "dialogue" is the tutorial AI and the Warden reacting to what Pilot *does* (shooting or holding fire). That keeps the VO file small and makes the pacifist choice the character's only voice.
- **[Featureless Protagonist](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/FeaturelessProtagonist).** A player character deliberately left without characterization, so the player can occupy it. Example: Zork: Grand Inquisitor's "AFGNCAAP" joke *(on page)*. **Subvert.** Pilot looks featureless (helmet, no face, the modeled cockpit seat "nobody will ever see"), but the flashback reveals the featurelessness is structural: Pilot is a role filled by whoever or whatever is at the controls. The blankness is the plot, not a convenience.
- **[Only Known by Their Nickname](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/OnlyKnownByTheirNickname)** and **[Everyone Calls Him "Barkeep"](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/EveryoneCallsHimBarkeep).** The first is a character known only by a nickname. The second (the closer fit) is a character known only by job title. **Use** the Barkeep version. "Pilot" is a job title, and the code's `Pilot` interface makes it literally one. See the name evaluation below.
- **[Player Character](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/PlayerCharacter).** The entity the player controls, which can sit anywhere from a full character to a cursor. **Use** as the theme: the original's `Pilot = KeyboardPilot | AutoPilot` split turns the question of who the player character is into a story beat.
- **[Hello, \[Insert Name Here\]](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/HelloInsertNameHere).** The player names the protagonist and the game slots the name in. **Subvert.** The new-game flow shows a lavish name-entry screen with the field pre-filled "PILOT" and locked, with a tooltip: "Callsign assigned by Lane Authority." It costs one UI screen, shows off front-end polish, and plants the identity theme.

### 2. Mentors, guides and the tutorial AI

- **[Exposition Fairy](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/ExpositionFairy).** A companion who explains the interface and the plot, often too much. Examples: Navi; Fi; Peppy Hare's barrel-roll advice in Star Fox; Adam in Metroid Fusion, who later turns out to have an agenda *(all on page)*. **Use, then Subvert.** The tutorial AI is the game's only friendly voice, and the Fusion and Metroid Dread pattern (the helper serves someone else) is the twist to borrow.
- **[Mission Control](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/MissionControl)** and **[Voice with an Internet Connection](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/VoiceWithAnInternetConnection).** A remote handler in the hero's ear who sees maps and screens the hero cannot. Examples: Metal Gear's Codec cast; inFAMOUS 2's Zeke *(on page quote)*. **Use.** The tutorial AI stays on comms throughout gameplay and the boss fight, so it can react to the flashback and the ending. It should talk less after the tutorial, and that drop is itself characterization.
- **[Annoying Video Game Helper](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/AnnoyingVideoGameHelper)** (via the [Stop Helping Me!](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/StopHelpingMe) disambiguation; not fetched). A helper who interrupts with useless help. **Use** in the tutorial only, with a hard handoff: the AI's last tutorial line admits it was "required to say all of that."
- **Escort-style mentors.** No single page was found. The mentor beat folds into the AI above.

### 3. Tutorial tropes (the knowingly overlong tutorial)

- **[Forced Tutorial](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/ForcedTutorial).** A tutorial the player cannot skip, even when they know how to play. Example: the Kingdom Hearts jump-tutorial gag *(on page)*. **Use**, honestly flagged: the tutorial is a required beat. Offer a "Skip tutorial" button that opens a confirmation, then a second confirmation, then a short "Skip tutorial tutorial". After a third refusal the AI relents ("Logged as: declined onboarding"), and that log feeds the ending. Make sure the real exit is at most three clicks away, so the joke never becomes an accessibility failure.
- **[Captain Obvious](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/CaptainObvious)** and **[Tutorial Failure](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/TutorialFailure).** A character who states the self-evident, and a tutorial that explains things badly. Example: Ace Combat 04's AWACS Sky Eye repeating what the player just did (search snippet of the Captain Obvious video-games subpage). **Use** Captain Obvious (the AI explains that bullets are bad, then that pink bullets are *also* bad). **Avoid** real Tutorial Failure: everything the AI teaches must be correct and must actually help with the boss, so the joke stays on the AI and not on the player.
- **[He Knows About Timed Hits](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/HeKnowsAboutTimedHits).** In-fiction characters name the real buttons. Examples: Mother 3's "something R-Button-ish" quote; Paper Mario; Metal Gear *(on page)*. **Use.** The AI reads key bindings from the live controls settings ("press SPACE, or whatever you have rebound it to; I can see that you rebound it"). That shows off the settings menu and costs nothing.
- **[Unnecessary Combat Roll](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/UnnecessaryCombatRoll).** Protagonists roll for no tactical reason. **Use as a gag, Avoid as a mechanic.** The tutorial teaches a barrel roll (Star Fox homage) that only plays the bank animation and has no i-frames. Later the AI admits: "Marketing asked for it." Keep the hitbox truth intact, because bullet-hell trust depends on it.
- **[Leaning on the Fourth Wall](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/LeaningOnTheFourthWall).** Dialogue that is plausibly in-world but obviously about the work itself. **Use** as the main comic register, rather than outright fourth-wall breaks. The AI talks about "onboarding metrics" and "retention" as in-world Lane Authority bureaucracy, which also describes the AAAA industry. This is the right dial for "self-aware, not a parody."

### 4. Flashback and memory

- **[Flashback](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/Flashback)** (no Playable Flashback page exists) and **[Flashback Cut](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/FlashbackCut).** A scene from before the main action; the Cut is a seconds-long snap to the past and back. **Use a short playable flashback, bracketed by Flashback Cuts.** On first pickup the game hard-cuts (1–2 s) to a desaturated cockpit, plays a 30–45 s flashback in which the player is briefly allowed to fly, then snaps back to the same frame. Freeze the world state during the flashback, so the beat costs the player nothing in score or lives.
- **[Trigger Phrase](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/TriggerPhrase)** (and trigger objects). A conditioned phrase or object that activates hidden behavior or memory. Example: BioShock *(on page)*. **Use** an object. The mint-green spread crystal is a recovered memory core (the original's three dots become three recorded ships), and picking it up plays back a previous Pilot's last flight. Optionally pair it with an AI line that becomes a phrase: "Stay in your lane, Pilot."
- **[Amnesiac Hero](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/AmnesiacHero)** and **[Laser-Guided Amnesia](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/LaserGuidedAmnesia).** A hero who has lost their past; amnesia that removes exactly what the plot needs removed. Examples: Planescape: Torment, Knights of the Old Republic, Final Fantasy VII *(on page)*. **Subvert.** Pilot is not amnesiac. Pilot never had the memory, because each Pilot is fresh (a new ship and a new instance). The flashback is someone else's memory, inherited through the pickup. That avoids the tired amnesia device and fits the continue system.

### 5. The Warden (boss)

- **[Large Ham](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/LargeHam).** A character who plays every moment at maximum volume and grandeur. Example: Metal Gear's bosses *(on page)*. **Use, with sincerity.** The Warden is operatic because it is old and alone and has rehearsed this speech for every Pilot who came before. The ham should be earnest, not winking, so the ending can turn it to pathos.
- **[Boss Vulnerability](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/BossVulnerability)** and **[Cutscene Power to the Max](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/CutscenePowerToTheMax).** Bosses that can only be damaged at certain times; characters far stronger in cutscenes than in play (Mass Effect 2's Jack, *on page*). **Use** the original's 150-tick invulnerable descent as diegetic speech time: the Warden will not be harmed while it is talking, and shots visibly splash off a "parley field." **Avoid** Cutscene Power: whatever the Warden does in cinematics, it must also do in the fight (same patterns, same colors), or the shmup loses its honesty.
- **[Boss Banter](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/BossBanter).** A boss that talks during combat. Examples: GLaDOS, Wheatley *(on page)*; Touhou's pre-spell-card dialogue *(from memory)* for the shmup precedent. **Use.** Phase transitions (when armor plates shed) each get one bark, and the bark depends on whether Pilot has killed any scouts.
- **[Turns Red](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/TurnsRed).** A boss that gets more aggressive at low HP. **Use**; it is already in the code (phase 3 spiral). Narratively, phase 3 is the Warden spending its reserves ("Everything they have" was already the sector 5 subtitle).
- **[Villainous Breakdown](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/VillainousBreakdown).** A villain who comes apart emotionally as their plan fails. **Subvert.** The Warden does not break down into rage. It breaks down into grief and starts counting the rings it has left, which pays off the original epilogue line "lights go dark, one ring at a time."
- **Not So Different** ([disambiguation](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/NotSoDifferent); see [Evil Counterpart](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/EvilCounterpart) and [Foil](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/Foil), not fetched). The antagonist mirrors the hero. **Use, but drop the "Evil".** The Warden was once a Pilot, or was built from one, and its drones are to it what continues are to the player.
- **[Talking the Monster to Death](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/TalkingTheMonsterToDeath).** Beating a monster with words instead of weapons (Fallout's speech-check bosses, *on page*). **Invert** for the pacifist run. Pilot cannot talk (Heroic Mime), so Pilot "argues" by not firing, and the *Warden* talks itself out of the fight.

### 6. Moral ending and the pacifist run

- **[Pacifist Run](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/PacifistRun).** A self-imposed or supported playthrough without killing. Examples: Undertale, Deus Ex, Dishonored, Metal Gear *(on page)*; Ikaruga's no-firing "bullet eater" clears *(from memory)* for the shmup precedent. **Use,** and make it mechanically real but cheap: a run with zero kills through Sector 5 changes Warden dialogue, turns the fight into a survival timer (dodge its full pattern cycle), and plays an alternate ending. Kill-based scoring then makes the pacifist score 0000000, which the end card can frame as the true high score.
- **[Video Game Cruelty Punishment](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/VideogameCrueltyPunishment).** Games that punish gratuitous cruelty. Examples: Dishonored's Chaos system, Undertale *(on page)*. **Subvert.** Nothing in the run punishes killing (the score rewards it), and that silence is the point. The ending's tally of "scouts lost: 162" (the self-play run's real kill count) is the only consequence.
- **[Gameplay and Story Segregation](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/GameplayAndStorySegregation)** (Ludonarrative Dissonance redirects here). Gameplay rules and story contradict each other. **Avoid** accidental cases and **Use** one deliberate case. The 2D game rewarded kills with points; the ending reinterprets those points as a body count. Every other system (continues, scouts, invulnerable descent) gets a diegetic reason.
- **[Shoot the Shaggy Dog](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/ShootTheShaggyDog).** The protagonist's achievement turns out to be pointless or self-defeating. Examples *(from memory)*: NieR's endings, Spec Ops: The Line. **Use lightly.** Opening the lanes "works", but it serves someone else. Avoid full nihilism: the pacifist route must offer real hope, or the motivation to replay collapses.
- **[Grey-and-Gray Morality](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/GreyAndGrayMorality).** Both sides have defensible reasons and mixed members. **Use, but name the power asymmetry.** The Warden really did block travel, and people really did need the lanes. The gray is not even: an institution with enormous resources spent disposable Pilots to break a guardian of a commons.
- **[Was It Really Worth It?](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/WasItReallyWorthIt).** Victory whose cost makes it hollow. Examples *(from memory)*: Shadow of the Colossus, Spec Ops: The Line. **Use.** This is the emotional target of the ending text.
- **[You Bastard!](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/YouBastard).** The work blames the audience for enjoying its violence. **Avoid the accusation, keep the mirror.** "Not a parody" means no sneering at the player. The ending shows the consequences in-world and lets the player draw the conclusion, then gently points at the pacifist route. The original's "THANK YOU FOR PLAYING" stays sincere, with the tally under it.
- **[Hero Antagonist](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/HeroAntagonist)** and **[Villain Protagonist](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/VillainProtagonist)** (no "You Are the Villain" page). An antagonist who is the good guy; a protagonist who is the bad guy. **Use Hero Antagonist** (the Warden). **Avoid Villain Protagonist.** Pilot is not villainous. Pilot is deployed: complicit, not evil. That keeps blame on the institution.
- **[Barrier Maiden](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/BarrierMaiden)** (substitute for the missing Gatekeeper/Guardian pages). A character whose continued existence holds a protective barrier in place. Examples: Final Fantasy X's Yuna, Zelda *(on page)*. **Use** for the Warden. The rings around its core are the barrier, and each ring that goes dark opens a lane. What it holds back depends on the spine.
- **[Multiple Endings](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/MultipleEndings)** and **[Golden Ending](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/GoldenEnding).** Branching finales, and the best one among them. Examples: Undertale, NieR, Silent Hill *(on page)*. **Use two endings** (Lanes Open, and Lanes Kept). The pacifist one is the Golden Ending. Do not build a third neutral ending in the slice.
- **[New Game Plus](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/NewGamePlus).** Replay with carried-over state. Examples: Chrono Trigger, NieR, Undertale *(on page)*. **Use minimally.** After the first ending the title menu gains "Fly again (Pilot #0123)", the serial increments, and the tutorial AI's first line changes ("Welcome back. Or, welcome. Records are unclear."). The skip button then works on one click.
- **[And Your Reward Is Clothes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/AndYourRewardIsClothes).** Post-game rewards that are only cosmetic. **Subvert:** the pacifist run unlocks one cosmetic, the Warden's crimson trim on Pilot's ship, and it means something.
- **Undertale-style routes.** No genocide-route Main page exists. Undertale's own trope subpage ([Undertale/TropesB](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Undertale/TropesB), via search snippet) notes that the No Mercy and Genocide names refer to the same officially unnamed route. **Avoid** a genocide route. The default run already is one in miniature (killing everything that moves is just the shmup), so the slice needs only the contrast with pacifism.

### 7. Shmup-specific

- **[Bullet Hell](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/BulletHell)** and **[Shoot 'em Up](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/ShootEmUp).** Dense, patterned enemy fire with tiny hitboxes; the parent genre of lone ships against endless waves. Examples: Touhou, DoDonPachi, Ikaruga, Mushihimesama *(on page)*. The Shoot 'em Up page jokes that the genre's standard premise is sending one lone hero against an armada, which is the premise this slice questions. **Use.**
- **Bullet-hell lore** *(from memory; no single page)*. Shmup stories are usually thin, but a few famous twists exist. DoDonPachi's commander reveals you were shooting down your own forces to breed the perfect soldier. ZeroRanger and NieR: Automata use shmup play for loops and moral reversal. Undertale fuses bullet-hell dodging with sparing. **Use** the DoDonPachi precedent: the "enemies" were never an invading force.
- **[Continuing Is Painful](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/ContinuingIsPainful).** Continues carry a penalty. Examples: Touhou, Gradius *(on page)*. **Subvert.** Continuing costs the player nothing mechanically, but costs a Pilot: the continue screen reads "Requisitioning Pilot #0123" with an in-world price. That is the painful part.
- **[Justified Extra Lives](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/JustifiedExtraLives), [Expendable Clone](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/ExpendableClone), [Clone Angst](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/CloneAngst), [Clones Are People, Too](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/ClonesArePeopleToo).** In-fiction reasons for lives, often clones or remote bodies (Assassin's Creed, BioShock's Vita-Chambers, *on page*); clones treated as replaceable; clones distressed about it; clones granted personhood. **Use** Justified Extra Lives as the backbone (the original autopilot "buys another ship"). **Avoid** on-screen Clone Angst, since a silent Pilot cannot emote it. **Use** Clones Are People, Too as the Warden's position: it is the only character that ever says a Pilot's serial number out loud like a name.
- **[The All-Seeing A.I.](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/TheAllSeeingAI)** (bonus). Game AI that cheats by reading the true state. The original `AutoPilot` reads predicted threat positions 0–20 ticks ahead. **Use** as codex flavor: the autopilot Pilots were superhuman because they read the Lane Authority's feed, not the sky.

### 8. Front end and meta (vertical-slice tropes)

- **Unskippable Cutscene** (redirects to [Cutscene](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/Cutscene)). **Avoid** for real: every cinematic is skippable after first view, and the logos are skippable after 2 s. The one exception is the Warden's descent speech, which is skippable but whose length is its invulnerability window, so skipping it just starts the fight. The overlong-tutorial joke carries the "tedium" beat so that cutscenes do not have to.
- **[Loading Screen](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/LoadingScreen)** tips. Text or minigames shown while content loads (Ridge Racer's Galaxian loader, Bayonetta, *on page*). **Use.** Tips start as gameplay advice and drift into lore and unease ("Tip: The Warden's scouts do not fire on ships that have not fired. This is not a tip."). If loads are too fast, show tips on the pause menu instead of faking load times.
- **[Credits Gag](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/CreditsGag).** Jokes inside the credits. Examples: Portal, Monty Python *(on page)*. **Use** once, quietly. The credits list "Pilot #0001 – #0122" as stunt performers, each with a role ("Pilot #0047: Shatter Belt, 00:41"). In the pacifist ending that block is replaced by "Pilot #0122 (still flying)".
- **[Dummied Out](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/DummiedOut).** Content left in the files but made unreachable. **Use** the original's real cut content as in-world archaeology: the dart's commented-out gun ("darts were disarmed by treaty"), the never-played music cue as the Warden's own theme, and the `bossDefeted` typo as a codex easter egg. This keeps the reference project teachable for students too.
- **[Vaporware](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/Vaporware).** Software endlessly promised and never shipped. **Use** sparingly in the stubbed features ("Multiplayer: coming in the f27 cohort"). **Avoid** stubbing the pacifist ending itself: the slice's thesis is that the pacifist run exists, so a "not included" card there would undercut the whole ending.
- **[This Is Gonna Suck](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/ThisIsGonnaSuck)** (bonus). A character resigned to an ordeal. **Use** once, as the tutorial AI's "WARNING / The Warden approaches" line.

## Findings: three narrative spines

All three keep the original's text intact ("The lanes stay closed", "The lanes are open", the three epilogue lines) and reinterpret it.

### Spine A: "Enclosure" (the Warden guards a commons)

- **Premise.** The lanes are free travel corridors through a nebula, held open for centuries by the Warden for anyone who flies without weapons. The Lane Authority (a chartered toll consortium, with in-world branding that rhymes with the CM121 incubator) wants them enclosed. It cannot win a war of attrition, so it sends Pilots: cheap, serialized, remotely reinstanced. The tutorial AI is the Authority's onboarding system.
- **Flashback (first powerup).** The spread crystal is the recovered flight recorder of Pilot #0121, your immediate predecessor, who reached the Warden, stopped firing, and was recalled (terminated) by the Authority for "non-performance." For a few seconds you fly #0121's final approach: the scouts stop shooting at you, and the Warden speaks to you by number. The cut back reveals your HUD serial: **Pilot #0122**.
- **The Warden's true role.** A Barrier Maiden and Hero Antagonist. Its rings throttle armed traffic (hence the parley field), its drones are scouts that only fire on ships that have fired, and the lanes it "closes" are closed only to armed traffic.
- **Ending (kill run).** Over the original epilogue lines: "The Warden's lights go dark, one ring at a time." / "Behind you, five sectors of open sky." / Tollgates unfold across the open sky, Authority logos blooming in the nebula. / "Ahead, the long way home." / AI: "Congratulations, Pilot #0122. Your contract is complete. Your replacement is already in onboarding." / Tally: "Scouts lost: N. Pilots spent: 122. Lanes: open (subscription required)." / "THANK YOU FOR PLAYING."
- **Pacifist run unlocks.** The Warden's dialogue tree completes, the survival-timer fight, and the "Lanes Kept" ending: the Warden recognizes #0122, cuts the recall link, and Pilot takes the long way home *unowned*. Cosmetic: the crimson trim.
- **Tradeoffs.** Strongest thematic payload (enclosure of commons, disposable labor, VC-style capture) with legible power relations, and it reuses every original hook. Risk: preachiness. Mitigate through the Warden's sincerity and by keeping the Authority banal rather than cartoonish.

### Spine B: "Succession" (the Warden was a Pilot)

- **Premise.** Pilots are trained to break through the lanes to "home." Nobody ever has.
- **Flashback.** The memory core is the Warden's: you see the first Pilot, centuries ago, choosing to stop and hold the lanes against something beyond them.
- **The Warden's true role.** A former Pilot fused with the barrier, holding back an unnamed void-front, so the lanes are closed for everyone's safety. Not So Different, played sincerely.
- **Ending (kill run).** The lanes open, and something vast stirs beyond Sector 6. Pilot is pulled into the Warden's empty crest; the last frame shows Pilot's cyan trim turning crimson. "Ahead, the long way home" becomes the new Warden's vigil. A cycle, near Shoot the Shaggy Dog.
- **Pacifist run unlocks.** The old Warden passes the watch on *without dying*, and the two share it.
- **Tradeoffs.** Mythic and elegiac, a good fit for over-acted grief. But the "thing beyond" is an external evil, which flattens the gray into "the Warden was right, you were wrong," a simpler moral than the brief asks for, and it needs one more invented entity.

### Spine C: "Attract Mode" (Pilot is the autopilot)

- **Premise.** Leaning hardest on the code: the ship has always been flown by `AutoPilot`. The player is a "supervisor" the system assumes is present.
- **Flashback.** The memory core shows the title screen's self-play demo from the inside: thousands of deterministic runs (seed 121), each ending with "buys a continue."
- **The Warden's true role.** The only entity in the game that knows the difference between a person and an autopilot, and the only one that wants a person.
- **Ending (kill run).** "Final score 0035000. Self-play: continues=1." The Warden asks, "Was anyone flying?"
- **Pacifist run unlocks.** Proof of a human at the controls, because the autopilot is incapable of not firing.
- **Tradeoffs.** Clever and very teachable (the code's architecture becomes the plot), but it is the most meta, and it slides toward parody and the You Bastard register. It also makes moral weight hinge on a technicality.

### Recommendation

**Spine A ("Enclosure"), borrowing one element from C:** the attract mode's self-play is diegetically "Pilot #0121's recorded runs," so the title-screen demo foreshadows the flashback. A fits the brief best: the gray rests on a real conflict of interests rather than a hidden monster, the pacifist run is an act of refusal rather than a puzzle, and every original line, mechanic and bug gets a reason. It is also the most honest about power: the Warden is a guardian of a commons, Pilots are disposable labor, and the "victory" is a privatization. That gives the slice moral stakes without moralizing at the player.

For the Titanfall collision, the serial and the crimson-versus-cyan visual identity carry enough distinction for a vertical slice; revisit only if this ever ships commercially.

## Caveats

- Example lists were not read exhaustively, and many examples are from memory (flagged). TV Tropes pages change frequently and several trope names have been retired or redirected (listed above).
- The pacifist mechanic (zero kills through Sector 5, survival timer at the Warden) has not been prototyped. The Warden's 900 HP and phase gating are HP-based in the original, so the pacifist fight needs time-based phase advancement. That is a small code change, but untested.
- Spine A's institutional critique depends on tone in VO delivery. The Authority should sound helpful and banal, never sinister, or the gray collapses into black and white.
