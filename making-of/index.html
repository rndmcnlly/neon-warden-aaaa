Making of Neon Warden AAAA

Written October 29, 2026, from docs/MAKING-OF-STORY.md, the story Adam approved. The landing page has the short version.

The seed

Adam had just written the starter code for a refactoring exercise sequence in his software engineering course (CMPM 121, UC Santa Cruz): a deliberately messy little web shoot-'em-up in Phaser, small and intentionally smelly, so students have something worth refactoring. It is public: ucsc-cmpm-121-f26/e4-starter, and a read-only copy lives in this repository under reference/e4-starter/.

The question

What would a AAAA adaptation of this silly example shmup look like, honestly, as a vertical slice: the thing a studio shows publishers and funders to argue that a game should exist, not a product for consumers? Neon Warden AAAA is where that led after about a day of cooking and transagentic skill cultivation: the human and the agents forming one acting entity, each round leaving behind reusable practice (repo-local skills, QA tooling, judge workflows).

Adam's framing: a meta vertical slice of next-gen AI-assisted game production. A vertical slice communicates a future game to funders; this one communicates a future way of making games to students, while showing how a label like AAAA measures capital rather than craft.

Timeline, for any claim: first commit 2026-10-28 18:17, about 150 commits through 2026-10-29 midday (roughly 17 hours of wall-clock time, including an overnight unattended run). Earlier exploration happened in a separate course workspace on October 28.

Where "AAAA" fits

Stepico's explainer calls AAAA an unofficial, often self-assigned marketing label and compares AAA and AAAA games on five dimensions. Neon Warden AAAA inverts nearly all of them. Only one axis is pushed to the extreme, and it is not in their table but in a bullet on AI-assisted content generation.

The method

A main agent session orchestrated background workers, each owning named files, with contracts pinned before code. Local models made the assets. Judge panels were primed with concerns drawn from cited web sources. Short QA reels with real audio served as dailies, and blind checks sometimes said a night of fixes had made things worse (the judge preferred the before build in 7 of 10 pairs; we cut the change and did not claim an improvement).

The cultivation

Failures became written rules for the next agents: write shared files atomically (a status file was once truncated by a concurrent write), review blind before revealing the truth, and vary the written brief rather than the random seed when you want real alternatives. These live as repo-local skills in .opencode/skills/, with their evidence in docs/SKILLS.md.

Who did what

Adam Smith directed: story, scope cuts, casting by ear in context, playtests and every final call. The credits read "Adam Smith, and his coupled agents".

Agents. Orchestration and implementation ran mostly on Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic). For a segment, work moved to GPT-6.1 Sol (OpenAI) because the Claude subscription hit its 5-hour usage window; that window was exhausted largely by an unrelated, ambitious project running in parallel, not by Neon Warden alone. Work then returned to Claude. The credits name both models.

Judge and small passes. Gemini 4 Argon (Google) through OpenRouter, with zero data retention required, as the blind audiovisual judge. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and GLM-5.3-Flash for research summaries and compressing agent instructions.

Asset models. Local on one laptop: Qwen3-TTS for every voice line, Qwen3-ASR to check renders against the script, Stable Audio 3 for music and interface sounds. In the cloud: ElevenLabs Sound Effects v2 via fal (paid) for gameplay impacts and weapons; those files cannot be regenerated, so they are committed.

Cost

About $7 in all. About $2.25 of it is metered API calls, every one logged in the public spend ledger: OpenRouter (mostly blind judge passes) about $2.01, fal (cloud sound effects) about $0.24, and a cent of attested-enclave inference. The rest is an estimated share of AI subscriptions, which do not bill per project.

Energy, roughly

Adam's laptop (MacBook Pro M3 Max) had its heaviest day of CPU and GPU use ever, but with a low duty cycle: peaks near 120 W during local inference (voices, speech checks, music, Blender, frame-stepped captures) against about 8 W while idle, waiting on remote inference or on human review. Over roughly 17 hours, any plausible average between 15 and 40 W puts local energy at about 0.25–0.7 kWh. That is an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a measurement.

The larger share is invisible from here: datacenter inference for the orchestration agents and judges, which this project cannot measure. So these local watts are not the footprint.

The result, and its licenses

A parked, pedagogical artifact students can read, play and question: a static site with nothing to keep alive. Code is MIT; art, audio and text are CC BY 4.0; the logo vectors are CC0. Third party software, fonts and models are listed in the README and in the game (Extras, Third-Party Licenses).
